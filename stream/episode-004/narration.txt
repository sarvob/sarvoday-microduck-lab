How long should a robot keep rolling before it asks its balance controller to
take over? A few hundredths of a second can separate a clean recovery from a
second uncontrolled rotation. In this episode, we turn that handoff into a
measurable robotics problem, search the timing, and test the result with three
perturbed starting poses.

The robot is Microduck. We use the official roulade network shipped with its
open simulator. That joint-level neural policy remains frozen. We are not
retraining the motion or claiming a new rolling network. The component we learn
is smaller and easier to inspect: the exact moment when control changes from
roulade to the official standing policy.

First, we need to define success. A dramatic animation is not enough. The trunk
must accumulate at least three hundred degrees of rotation. Its upright score
must fall below minus zero point eight, proving that the body reached a real
inverted orientation. After recovery, the average upright score must be at
least zero point nine. Finally, the duck must stop within twenty centimetres of
its starting point. All of those conditions must pass for three different
initial joint perturbations.

The simulator runs its controller at fifty hertz, so one control step is twenty
milliseconds. We search every handoff from thirty-five through sixty steps.
That covers zero point seven seconds through one point two seconds and produces
twenty-six candidates. Each candidate is evaluated on seeds twenty-three,
seventy-nine, and one hundred ninety-one. The seeds add small joint-angle noise
before the robot settles, exposing timing choices that work only from one exact
pose.

The early timings are surprisingly capable. Handoffs from thirty-five through
forty-one steps pass all three seeds. But the margin changes across the range,
so we rank successful candidates by worst-case displacement and final balance.
At forty-one steps, or zero point eight two seconds, the worst drift is eleven
point eight five centimetres and every final upright score is one point zero.

One step later, the behavior becomes fragile. At forty-two steps, seed
twenty-three keeps rotating past the useful landing window. It accumulates more
than six hundred fifty degrees and finishes with an upright score of zero point
eight one eight, below the required zero point nine. That single twenty
millisecond delay turns a three-out-of-three controller into a two-out-of-three
controller. Longer handoffs often produce extra rotations, poor recovery, or
drift well beyond twenty centimetres.

Now look at the selected timing from two cameras. The overview shows the full
body path and makes displacement easy to judge. The inset comes from the
robot's physical head camera. During the roll, that view rotates with the head,
which makes the control transition visible in a way a fixed cinematic camera
cannot. We keep the graphics deliberately quiet so the motion remains the
evidence.

Seed twenty-three completes three hundred forty-four point one degrees of body
rotation and stops eleven point eight five centimetres away. Seed seventy-nine
completes three hundred thirty-five point three degrees and stops ten point nine
eight centimetres away. Seed one hundred ninety-one completes three hundred
thirty point one degrees and stops ten point four two centimetres away. Every
run reaches inversion, returns to a final upright score of one point zero, and
passes the displacement limit.

The lesson is not that forty-one steps is a universal number. It is specific to
this robot, these policies, this simulator, and this control rate. The broader
lesson is that transitions between competent controllers are themselves a
control problem. Define physical gates, search at the system's real timing
resolution, test perturbations, and inspect the boundary where success first
becomes unstable.

The challenge specification, all twenty-six candidate results, the selected
policy, evaluation seeds, tests, and rendering script are available in the
Sarvoday Robotics repository. The next step is to carry the same discipline
into locomotion: replace feet with rollers and ask Microduck to skate with a
measurable speed, stopping distance, and stability margin.
