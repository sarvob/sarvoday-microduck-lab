Can a small biped robot land on a quadruped and stay balanced? It looks like a
simple jump. In simulation, it is really three problems: reaching the target,
placing both feet, and rejecting the impact without falling. In this episode,
we will build each part, show what failed, and evaluate the final controller on
three launches it did not train on.

The scene uses the open Microduck model and the pinned Unitree Go1 from the
MuJoCo Menagerie. The landing target is sixty centimetres forward and forty-one
point seven centimetres above the duck's starting position. We added a thin
cyan pad only as a contact sensor and visual target. It does not pull the robot
in, and the quadruped is fixed so this experiment isolates the biped's landing.

Before training anything, we asked whether the stock Microduck behavior could
physically reach that height. The shipped roll policy moved forward, but its
trunk rose only seven point three seven centimetres. It was truly airborne for
two hundredths of a second. The target requires forty-one point seven
centimetres. Calling that an unassisted jump would be misleading, so we rejected
the original claim instead of hiding the limitation.

That measurement changes the question. We now apply one disclosed initial
velocity: one point two metres per second forward and three point three metres
per second upward. Think of it as a reproducible launch rig. It acts once, at
the first instant. After that, there is no external force in flight. The robot
must control its own pose, find the platform with both feet, recover upright,
and remain there.

We calibrated the launch with an eighty-one-point ballistic grid. Each
candidate combined a forward and vertical velocity. The objective rewarded
reaching the pad and penalized unnecessary speed. The best launch reached the
target with a measured vertical landing speed of zero point two one eight
metres per second. But reachability was not enough. With the stock stand
controller, the duck held for only zero point nine six seconds, below our one
point five second success gate.

The learned component is deliberately small. We keep the proven neural stand
policy frozen and add an eight-parameter residual: four symmetric posture terms
for flight and four for the landing phase. Those terms adjust hip, knee, ankle,
and stance width. A small controller is easier to inspect, faster to search,
and harder to mistake for magic.

Training uses cross-entropy search. In every generation, forty candidate
residuals are tested. The best eight define the next distribution. Sixteen
generations are enough for the score to rise from twenty-nine point three to
thirty-nine point four. More importantly, the stable hold improves beyond the
gate. The score is only a search tool; success is decided by explicit physical
conditions.

Those conditions are strict. Both feet must contact the Go1 pad at the same
time. The torso must remain upright. The duck must hold for at least one point
five seconds. And after reaching the pad, no part of the duck may touch the
floor. A lucky single rollout does not count.

Now we test three randomized launches. Seed seventeen changes the initial
velocity slightly and holds for one point six eight seconds. Seed seventy-one
lands with both feet and holds for one point six four seconds. Seed one hundred
seventy-three introduces a different horizontal error and also holds for one
point six four seconds. All three avoid later ground contact.

What did we actually learn? First, feasibility checks should happen before
long training runs. Second, assistance is acceptable only when it is disclosed
and separated from the learned behavior. Third, repeatable evaluation matters
more than a dramatic single clip. The result is not an autonomous jumping
robot. It is a verified landing controller following a controlled launch.

The complete controller weights, launch measurements, evaluation seeds, scene
builder, and machine-readable results are public in the Sarvoday Robotics
repository. The next challenge is harder: unpin the quadruped, make it move,
and ask Microduck to land on a target that does not stay still.
